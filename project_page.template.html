How much does the oil cost the J-damper?

A fluid inerter makes a suspension "feel" a large mass by forcing oil through a long helical channel. The ideal theory says it improves grip. This project uses CFD of the oil flow in the channel to measure what the real device does, and how much of the grip benefit survives on a race car.

Background

The device

An inerter is a suspension element whose force is proportional to the relative acceleration of its ends, F = b·ẍ, with the inertance b in kilograms (Smith, 2002). McLaren raced one in Formula 1 in 2005–2007 under the codename "J-damper".

In a fluid inerter, a 40 mm piston pushes ISO VG 32 oil through a 6 mm channel coiled 10 times (1.89 m). The oil in the channel moves 44× faster than the piston, so 46 g of oil acts like 91.7 kg of inertance.

The ideal model ignores the fluid dynamics: viscous friction, the secondary Dean vortices that the coil's curvature creates, and the way an oscillating flow's velocity profile changes with frequency (Womersley number). Those effects are what this CFD study measures.

Watch the slice when the flow is fastest. The fast oil is thrown against the outer wall of the coil, and the vortices strengthen. That stirring flattens the velocity profile, which is why the measured inertance is close to ideal while the damping is 2–3× a straight pipe. At flow reversal the vortices collapse and re-form.

Method

Workflow

1

Mesh

Own Python generator: structured O-grid hex mesh swept along the helix, 0.16M–0.90M cells, written directly in OpenFOAM format.

2

Validate

Steady laminar flow (simpleFoam) vs curved-pipe correlations, a 3-mesh GCI study, and a pitch check.

3

Oscillate

Periodic 10° torus sector, oscillating pressure drive at 1–20 Hz (pimpleFoam), giving the real inertance and damping.

4

Race car

Quarter-car on an ISO 8608 road at 180 km/h. Suspension layout optimised for grip with each inerter model.

All tools are free and open source: OpenFOAM v2406 (in WSL Ubuntu) and Python (NumPy, SciPy, Matplotlib).

What was built

The models

Every geometry and mesh was generated by the project's own Python code (make_helix_mesh.py), written straight into OpenFOAM's format and verified with OpenFOAM's checkMesh ("Mesh OK").

Also built: the inerter physics model (ideal inertance, curved-pipe friction, Womersley oscillating flow, cavitation check) and the reduced-order model F = b·ẍ + c₁·ẋ + c₂·ẋ|ẋ| fitted to the CFD, in python/inerter_model.py.

Result 1 · Validation

The CFD matches the correlations

Curvature makes the channel lose more pressure than a straight pipe. The ratio fc/fs grows with the Dean number De = Re·√(r/R).

The full-helix CFD agrees with Ito (1959) to within 0.3–3.6 % from Re = 100 to 4000, and with White (1929) to within about 3 % from Re = 250 up.

A periodic torus sector with no helix pitch (pink triangles) matches the full helix to within 0.1 % wherever it converged, so the pitch is negligible. That justifies running the oscillating cases on 2,560 cells instead of 237,000.

Result 2 · Oscillating flow

The real inerter is not ideal

Each case drives the oil back and forth at a fixed frequency and stroke. A Fourier analysis of force and piston velocity gives the equivalent inertance beq and parasitic damping ceq.

Inertance. A straight laminar pipe would read 1.22–1.33 × the ideal value (Womersley's exact solution, black line), because the parabolic velocity profile carries extra momentum. In the coil, the Dean vortices flatten the profile, bringing the inertance down to 1.02–1.26 ×. More stroke means stronger vortices and a value closer to ideal.

Damping. 1.1–3.2 × the straight-pipe value, growing with frequency and stroke. A quasi-steady curved-pipe correlation predicts it to within about 4 %. The inertance, however, is only captured by the CFD.

All 10 cases are fully periodic (cycle-to-cycle change ≤ 0.02 %).

Result 3 · Race car

What survives on track

Quarter-car model, GT/prototype corner, ISO 8608 class A road at 180 km/h. The metric is the RMS dynamic tyre load: less fluctuation means more consistent grip.

An inerter in parallel with the spring and damper gives no benefit. The benefit appears only in a J-damper style series branch (spring–damper–inerter in series, in parallel with the main spring), with every element re-optimised for each inerter model.

With the CFD-measured inerter, about 40–50 % of the ideal benefit is lost to the oil. Larger strokes lose more.

Result 4 · The piston and cavitation

Inside the piston chamber

The coil CFD drives the oil in the channel directly, because the oil is incompressible: piston area × piston speed = channel area × oil speed. The piston, its two oil chambers and the sharp-edged ports were simulated separately, as an axisymmetric CFD with a moving-piston boundary. The tube end is coupled to the rest of the coil through the inertance and damping measured by the coil CFD.

Port losses. The entry loss is K = 0.32–0.54. The exit loss is K = 1.9, not the textbook 1.0: laminar oil leaves the channel with a peaked profile carrying twice the kinetic energy, and the chamber destroys all of it.

Oscillating piston (10 Hz). The jet from the port rolls up into a ring vortex in the chamber. Chambers and ports add +7.4 % damping to the channel, with no real change in inertance.

The drilled port in 3D. In the device the port is a radial hole in the bore wall. It meets an axial drilling at a sharp corner and turns sharply again into the coil. A 3D CFD of that path, with fully developed coil flow arriving at the port, gives K_in ≈ 2.6 and K_out ≈ 3.8: about 6.4 velocity heads in total, against 2.3 for the port alone. Each sharp elbow costs about one velocity head, so radiused passages would be worth having.

Effect on grip: small. The vehicle model now uses these 3D losses. At a 2 mm stroke the ports add about 630 N s/m of damping at 10 Hz, rising in proportion to frequency. The quarter-car grip benefit stays at 5.0 %, because the tuning takes a slightly smaller inertance. With the J-dampers retuned, the track results move by up to ±1 percentage point.

Cavitation (two-phase CFD, Schnerr–Sauer model, 20 Hz). Vapour forms in the ring-vortex core and at the piston corner when the pre-charge is below ≈ 1.75 MPa. The hand calculation predicted 1.73 MPa.

Result 5 · Real cars on real tracks

The trade-off on track

A full-car model (body heave, pitch and roll, four wheels, anti-roll bars, bump stops, anti-dive) drives a Toyota GR Supra (road and GT4 race) and a Ford F-150 around Monza and Spa (satellite-measured centrelines), the Daytona oval (31° banking) and a straight rough test road. Each lap runs three suspensions on the same road at once.

On the straight test road every car reproduces the tuned gain (about +6–7 %). On circuits, braking and cornering transients excite body pitch and roll. Removing the main damper then costs grip, so the J-damper is a trade-off between bump grip and body control.

The surprise: on the soft road Supra and the F-150, the real inerter's fluid damping helps on track (+4 to +7 %), often beating the ideal inerter. On the stiff GT4, the fluid losses at higher frequencies cancel most of the gain (+0 to +2 %).

Scope

Limitations and next steps

  • Laminar, isothermal oilThe oil viscosity changes with temperature. Every case stays below the curved-pipe transition Reynolds number (about 9,300).
  • Chamber CFD is axisymmetricThe piston chamber and port are 2D, with a straight tube standing for the start of the coil.
  • Simple cavitation modelSchnerr–Sauer with a 2 kPa vapour/air-release pressure; dissolved-air release is not modelled in detail.
  • Track simulator scopeVertical tyre model only; quasi-steady lap profile; spring rates from owner forums; CoG, anti-roll bars and aero estimated.
  • High Dean number (De > 1000)Needs fine wall resolution: only the 0.9M-cell mesh converged there.
  • Experimental comparisonThe next validation step is the published fluid-inerter test data (Swift et al. 2013; Liu et al. 2018).
Reproduce

Run it yourself

From PowerShell in the project folder. All plots come from the existing results in about 2 minutes. The full CFD re-run takes about 1.5–2 hours on 8 cores.

powershell -ExecutionPolicy Bypass -File run_all.ps1 -SkipCFD
powershell -ExecutionPolicy Bypass -File run_all.ps1

Live flow demo in about 6 minutes: run the visualisation cases in Ubuntu, then render the animation in PowerShell.

bash "/mnt/c/Users/Rishi Kumar/Downloads/Pneumatics and Hydraulics/cfd/openfoam/run_in_wsl.sh" torus_demo
python cfd\scripts\render_demo.py

Step-by-step instructions are in HOW_TO_RUN.md, and the equations in docs/theory.md.
